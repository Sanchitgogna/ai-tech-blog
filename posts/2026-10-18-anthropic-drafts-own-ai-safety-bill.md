In Washington this week, the rarest creature in AI politics appeared: a frontier AI lab asking to be regulated.

The catch, of course, is that it's doing the asking with its own bill already drafted.

Anthropic has been quietly circulating a confidential draft federal AI-safety bill among congressional offices, [Politico first reported on Friday](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/info-tech/anthropic-quietly-pitches-its-own-ai-safety-bill-to-lawmakers-politico-reports/article71595297.ece), citing three people familiar with the effort who spoke anonymously about private deliberations. Anthropic confirmed the draft the same day. This isn't a leaked memo or a think-tank whitepaper — it's legislation, pre-written, handed to lawmakers by the industry it would govern.

So what's actually in it? Let's read the fine print, because the fine print is the story.

## The emergency brake

The headline provision is a narrow emergency brake. Under the draft, the government could block a model's release or use — but only if it poses a foreseeable, imminent risk of more than **$1 billion in damage**, or of **killing or seriously injuring more than 50 people**.

And even then, only three kinds of risk qualify: the creation or release of a biological weapon, a model slipping out of its developer's controls, or a cyberattack. Anything else — misinformation at scale, mass labour displacement, election interference, all the mid-tier nightmares people actually argue about — falls outside this framework entirely.

Also worth noting: who gets to pull the brake. Only the Commerce Department can even ask, and it can't act alone — it has to petition a federal judge in the District Court for the District of Columbia. Meanwhile, the draft explicitly prohibits *every other* US department, agency, or officer from suspending a model's development, deployment, or internal use.

This detail isn't academic. Over the summer, export controls reportedly sidelined two advanced Anthropic models — a shutdown by phone call, essentially, with no review. You can read the whole draft as a lab saying: fine, give the government an off-switch, but make it a judge with evidence, and take away everyone else's unreviewable version.

## The routine part is the serious part

Strip away the emergency brake and the rest of the draft is genuinely substantial governance, not theatre. Frontier labs would have to maintain safety frameworks, test their most powerful models, publish transparency reports for new releases, protect whistleblowers, and report critical incidents to Commerce. Independent evaluators would register with the Commerce Department and periodically assess both the labs and their frontier models. If an evaluator determines there's an "imminent enumerated risk," it has 72 hours to notify Commerce.

An Anthropic spokesperson said the bill turns proposals from the company's [Advanced AI Framework](https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/info-tech/anthropic-quietly-pitches-its-own-ai-safety-bill-to-lawmakers-politico-reports/article71595297.ece) — released earlier this year and advocated to federal and state lawmakers since June — into legal language. "These are sensible policies we believe Congress should act on," the spokesperson said.

All of this is reasonable. Mandatory testing, public disclosure, whistleblower protections: these are the things safety advocates have been begging for, and here they are, pre-drafted, from a lab's own pen.

## The dog that isn't barking

But a rule also tells you what it refuses to touch. Two silences are loud:

**First, the evaluators get paid by the labs they evaluate.** The draft allows frontier companies to compensate their independent reviewers, as long as the payment isn't tied to the evaluation's outcome. "As long as" is doing heroic work in that sentence. And this isn't hypothetical: last month, Anthropic named consultancy Accenture's Faculty unit as its first embedded safety evaluator — under a $1 billion contract. The cheque-book relationship is already the industry standard; the bill would enshrine it.

**Second, the draft preempts state law.** The bill would override certain state requirements covering public risk disclosures, independent evaluations, and critical-incident reporting. Read plainly: California, Illinois, and New York have been building stronger state-level rules on exactly these subjects, and a federal ceiling set at "imminent catastrophe" would knock them down. A bill pitched as pro-safety would, on day one, weaken the strictest protections that currently exist.

## A crowded pen

Anthropic's draft enters a legislative pile-up. The White House's AI task force adopted its charter this week and launched a 120-day review of federal AI risk management. Representatives Lori Trahan and Jay Obernolte have advanced the FRONTIER Act, which also overrides some state laws. In the Senate, Majority Leader John Thune is working with Amy Klobuchar and Ted Cruz on catastrophic-risk legislation, while safety advocates including Senator Maria Cantwell want a stronger federal review process. At the edges, Representative Greg Casar and Senator Bernie Sanders are calling for pausing advanced AI development and banning superintelligence. Outside Congress, the group Humans First is urging mandatory testing, and safety-focused PACs are now outraising the industry's own "Leading the Future" fund.

The one thing everyone agrees on is that *someone* should write the rules. The entire fight is over whose.

## The verdict

Here's the honest read: Anthropic's draft is the rare instance of the prisoner drafting the lock. The lock is real — testing mandates, transparency reports, whistleblower protections, an evaluator regime, and a court-ordered halt mechanism are all genuine constraints, and a judge weighing evidence is a material improvement over the phone calls that froze Anthropic's own models this summer. Right now there is no federal AI law at all, so a starting position from the lab most associated with safety culture is better than another year of slogans.

But the lock fits loosely, and it fits loosely on purpose. A $1-billion bar means everything short of catastrophe is, by definition, acceptable. The labs keep paying the graders. The states with the strongest rules get preempted. The government gets an off-switch, but only one department may hold it, only a judge may flip it, and only for three kinds of apocalypse.

Which, in the end, is the real story of AI regulation: the question was never whether the labs would be in the room. It's whether anyone else is holding a pen.
