Two years ago, Apple and OpenAI were partners. Apple put ChatGPT into the iPhone to prop up Apple Intelligence and make Siri smarter. On Friday, OpenAI sued Apple.

OpenAI filed an 81-page set of counterclaims in federal court in California, accusing Apple of stealing its trade secrets and "coercing and threatening" the suppliers building its first hardware. The Financial Times first reported the filing, quoting OpenAI's charge that Apple is trying to "compete unfairly by any means available."

The kicker: Apple sued OpenAI in July with almost exactly the same accusation, pointed the other way.

## The mirror-image fight

Apple's original lawsuit, filed in July, said OpenAI poached its employees — including former iPhone and Apple Watch design chief Tang Tan — to get at confidential hardware designs and information about unreleased products. The suit names OpenAI, its hardware unit io Products, and former Apple employees Chang Liu and Tang Tan.

OpenAI's answer is: no, you did it to us. Its counterclaims, filed by the same defendants, make three charges.

First, the trade secrets. OpenAI says a former operations employee — identified in the filing only as "Employee X" — sent more than 100 confidential files about unreleased OpenAI hardware to personal accounts before leaving on 2 June. After rejoining Apple, the employee allegedly briefed senior members of Apple's Asia-Pacific operations team in July about OpenAI's hardware plans, at Apple's request. The files reportedly included device images, engineering data and supplier information. OpenAI first raised this in an October filing. Apple said at the time that its investigation had found no OpenAI confidential information, and that it had asked for more specifics to keep looking.

Second, the suppliers. This is the part of the filing with the most colour. OpenAI alleges Apple asked shared suppliers to identify the staff assigned to its projects, and then pressed them to pull resources away. One manufacturer that had worked with OpenAI declined to continue, the filing says. Another moved OpenAI's anodising work to the night shift after Apple objected to it running alongside Apple's own production. Suppliers, according to the filing, "have expressed fear of losing Apple business and have moved or rescheduled meetings with OpenAI to avoid Apple seeing them."

Third, the employees. OpenAI accuses Apple of building its employment agreements into a trap: encouraging staff to keep work files on personal devices and personal iCloud and iMessage accounts, then using the presence of those files to threaten departing employees and discourage engineers from joining competitors. The filing argues Apple has applied its intellectual property agreements so broadly that they stop people taking jobs with rivals.

## What this is actually about

Strip out the legal language and this is a fight about the device after the phone.

OpenAI bought Jony Ive's hardware studio, io, last year for $6.4 billion, and has been building its first consumer devices ever since. Apple has spent the better part of two years watching a former partner become its most plausible challenger in hardware. The two companies now need the same suppliers, the same engineers, and the same manufacturing capacity — and OpenAI, a software company with no hardware supply chain of its own, has to build those relationships from scratch.

That asymmetry is the whole case. OpenAI's filing argues Apple is using its "size and wealth to forestall competition," including threatening suppliers that might work on its devices. The logic is straightforward: the incumbent doesn't need to win in court. It only needs the supplier to decide that the risk of losing Apple's business isn't worth the new customer.

Apple, for its part, has already won some ground. It sought a preliminary injunction to stop OpenAI using its alleged trade secrets, and OpenAI voluntarily agreed not to use them in October after a judge indicated he was likely to order that anyway. Apple has also won expanded access to evidence during discovery. The trial is scheduled for 19 July 2027.

## Keep the scoreboard honest

These are allegations, not findings. Apple has not commented on the new filing, and OpenAI denies Apple's allegations in its own answer. Both companies are accusing the other of the same conduct — stealing secrets through former employees — and one of them is wrong, or both of them are partly right, or the whole thing is just how talent moves between two companies that hire from the same small pool of hardware engineers.

That last possibility is the least exciting and the most likely. Hardware engineers in California move between companies constantly, and the line between "brought my expertise" and "took my former employer's secrets" is exactly what trade-secrets law exists to argue about.

But the supplier claims are different, and harder to dismiss. A night shift for anodising work is a specific, checkable detail. So is a manufacturer walking away. If those claims survive discovery, they stop being a hiring dispute and become something closer to an antitrust question: can the biggest buyer in the room tell its suppliers who else they're allowed to work for?

That's the question that will outlast this lawsuit. OpenAI's hardware is still unreleased and the trial is eight months away, but the fight is already doing its work: every supplier weighing whether to take OpenAI's call now has a very public reason to think twice. And that, whether the courts eventually call it unlawful or not, is exactly the outcome Apple is accused of wanting.
