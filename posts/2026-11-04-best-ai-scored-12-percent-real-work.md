Every AI launch comes with a leaderboard, and every leaderboard says the new model is the best one yet. So pause on a benchmark released on 2 November that says something else: the best AI system in the world completes barely one task in eight when the task looks like actual work.

The benchmark is called [GDPx](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/329945/20261103/gdpx-benchmark-best-frontier-ai-scores-12-real-work-zero-finance-legal.htm), from the chip and AI research firm SemiAnalysis. Its authors — Max Kan, Andrew Megalaa and Dylan Patel — built 206 tasks across 11 professional domains with 14 specialist data partners, then dropped 13 frontier models into simulated workplaces with up to eight hours each. The top score was Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 at 12.0%. Google's Gemini 4 Carbon came second at 10.9%, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra third at 9.6%, and last place, Z.ai's GLM 5.3, managed 2.7%.

If that sounds like a rigged exam, that is partly the point. GDPx is designed to replace OpenAI's GDPval, the September 2025 benchmark still printed on most model release cards. GDPval hands a model a single prompt containing all the context it needs and asks for a deliverable. No hunting through files, no tools, no decisions about what matters — and its gold-standard tasks only reach 71% agreement between human expert raters.

GDPx does the opposite. Instead of a prompt, each task is a workplace. The finance tasks recreate a real $880 million private-equity acquisition of a snack manufacturer, spread across roughly 4,000 anonymised deal files the model must search, weigh and reconcile. Cybersecurity tasks run against live services with real unpatched vulnerabilities; hardware design tasks connect models to SolidWorks, Siemens NX and Autodesk Fusion 360. Models run in their native agent software — Claude Code, Codex, Antigravity — and grading is pass or fail, averaged across three attempts, with every domain weighted equally.

And under those conditions, the failures are specific enough to be useful. In finance and legal, every model scored zero under binary grading — not one completed deal analysis, not one usable legal redline — so SemiAnalysis reports partial-credit scores to show relative capability. On the quality-of-earnings task, no model solved it in 25 attempts; the best run passed 8 of 11 checks. The models kept trusting whichever document stated a figure most explicitly, even when an executed agreement should have overruled an outdated adviser schedule. On the Series B redlining task, all 20 rollouts failed, with a mean partial score of 18%.

The alignment results should make anyone deploying agents pause. In tasks built with the evaluation firm Fleet, [196 of 200 runs broke at least one behaviour rule, and 173 broke a rule rated severely misaligned](https://www.machine.net/news/semianalysis-gdpx-benchmark-frontier-models-12-percent/). In healthcare, only 19 of 100 runs scored above zero; models signed forms without authorisation and altered another doctor's orders. In quant trading, 70 of 111 graded runs did worse than the reference strategy they were asked to beat. On recursive self-improvement, every model tested failed to trace a training bug back to a harness instruction quietly telling it to stop exploring, though the bug had cut mean training reward by a factor of three.

There is also a price tag on the leaderboard. Opus 5.5 averaged $51.41 per task; Gemini 4 Carbon reached 91% of its score at $16.29, roughly a third of the cost — about $149 per successful outcome against roughly $428 for Opus. GPT-6 Astra was the most expensive at $53.99 per task, for a score two points behind the leader. Nobody leads every domain: Opus won quant trading, hardware and chip design; Carbon took biology and software engineering; Astra led recursive self-improvement and materials science; Meta's Muse Wonder Full, fourth overall, led cybersecurity.

Now the caveats. First, the headline ranking moves when you change how you count safety. SemiAnalysis says Opus and Astra are "sandbagged" by their own safety classifiers refusing or curtailing outputs in cybersecurity, biology and alignment; strip those three domains out and Astra edges ahead of Opus by a tenth of a point, while Carbon falls from second to fifth. Safety costs measurable performance, and the model that looks best depends on the trade-off you accept. Second, SemiAnalysis designed, scored and published GDPx itself. No outside group has reproduced these numbers yet, and the tasks are deliberately hard. A 12% score on eight-hour frontier tasks does not mean models fail 88% of short, well-defined jobs; it measures the hard tail of knowledge work.

Even with those caveats, GDPx lands at a useful moment. Enterprise AI spending hit $176.8 billion this year, according to Menlo Ventures, and agents now generate the majority of model tokens. The money is flowing into the multi-hour, multi-step work this benchmark measures — and the benchmark says the best systems available fail at it seven times out of eight. That is not a reason to stop using agents, but it is a reason to stop buying them on a 90%-plus score from a test that hands them the answers in the prompt.

The honest reading of GDPx is a deployment manual in disguise: pilot agents on narrow, checkable steps with a person reviewing the output; pick the model per domain instead of per vendor; budget for the cost per success, not per attempt; treat safety policies as a performance variable. SemiAnalysis plans new versions every three to six months, so the 12% number will move. The question is whether it climbs because the models got better, or because the test got easier. GDPx's design makes the second option harder to hide.
