NVIDIA [reported its first results since Jensen Huang resigned](https://gamesbeat.com/nvidias-october-quarter-earnings-hit-115-8b-up-103-with-data-center-revenue-up-110/) on Tuesday, and they were the kind of numbers a board dreams about when its founder walks out the door.

Revenue hit $115.8 billion for the quarter to 25 October, up 103% on a year earlier. That is $7.8 billion more than NVIDIA itself forecast in August — the widest miss on its own guidance in five quarters. Data centre revenue rose 110% to $107.6 billion, and the next quarter is guided at $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, against roughly $125 billion that analysts expected. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $2.68, against forecasts of about $2.47. Net profit more than doubled to $65.3 billion.

Colette Kress, the chief financial officer running the company on an interim basis, called it "a clean quarter" in everything but words. "Our momentum is real," she said. "Vera Rubin is off to an explosive start, our roadmap is on track and our ambition has never been greater."

The shares rose about 2% in after-hours trading, to $159.47. That is the market's way of saying the machine works without the man who built it.

And it does. Vera Rubin NVL72 — NVIDIA's newest rack system — is shipping, and NVIDIA calls it the fastest product ramp in its history, with racks already running at CoreWeave, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, Oracle and Nebius. Edge computing, the bucket that holds PC, car and robotics chips, grew 42% to $8.2 billion. NVIDIA returned a record $28.1 billion to shareholders in the quarter.

So the first test is passed. But earnings reports are not just about the quarter. They are about what the company had to promise to produce it. And that is where this one gets interesting.

## The $390 billion bet

NVIDIA's commitments to buy from suppliers jumped to $390 billion at the end of the quarter, up from $279 billion three months earlier and $50.3 billion a year ago. Revenue doubled over the year; the commitments rose almost eightfold. Most of the increase, NVIDIA says, is "primarily related to the multi-year procurement of memory supply".

It is a company buying up years of memory before its rivals can, in a market where rising memory prices are already denting consumer PC demand.

Add it up and the total is starker still. All of NVIDIA's commitments reached $518 billion, up from $366 billion a quarter earlier. That includes $74 billion of data-centre leases that have not even started, with terms of up to twenty years. On top of that, NVIDIA has guaranteed up to $118 billion of other people's obligations — including $105 billion of credit support for an SB Energy campus in Ohio that is to be leased to OpenAI for twenty years, with the first building not expected in service until fiscal 2029.

None of this is hidden. It is all in the release. But it reframes what NVIDIA now is. It is not just the company that sells the picks and shovels of the AI boom. It is increasingly the company financing the mine.

Gross margin is guided down to 71–72% next quarter, from 74.8% now, as the Rubin ramp bites. None of these is alarming on its own — but together they are the cost of growing at triple digits: you pay in advance, you wait longer to get paid, and margins compress while the new hardware scales.

## The governance repair job

The board is also doing repair work in public. On the same day as the results, NVIDIA announced a new standing board committee for national security and export control oversight, chaired by director Suzanne Nora Johnson, with the company's existing national security and global trade compliance teams reporting directly to it. The company's cautionary statements also mention "the ongoing investigation by an independent committee of NVIDIA's board of directors" — an investigation neither the release nor the resignation announcement describes.

Huang, meanwhile, said on Tuesday morning that he would be willing to return if the board asked him. The Wall Street Journal reports that the board is not considering it.

That is the honest tension at the heart of this quarter. The business is performing better than it ever has. The market value has still slid from $5.5 trillion a month ago to $3.72 trillion today — a fall driven partly by the post-election sell-off in AI stocks, and partly by the fact that investors trusted one man's judgement about where this boom was heading more than they trust any spreadsheet.

Kress gave the board its strongest argument that the franchise can run without its founder: a quarter that beat its own forecast by nearly $8 billion. What she cannot give them, at least not yet, is the answer to the question the market will keep asking until a permanent chief executive is named — who decides what the next $390 billion gets spent on?

The numbers say NVIDIA is fine. The balance sheet says NVIDIA is now betting its own future on the boom continuing. Those are both true. The difference between them is the story of the next year.
