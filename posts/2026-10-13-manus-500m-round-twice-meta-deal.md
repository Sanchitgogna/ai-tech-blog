Here's a sentence you rarely read in tech: a government killed a startup's $2 billion exit, and the startup is doing fine.

Actually it's doing better than fine. Manus — the AI agent company Meta agreed to buy for roughly $2 billion last December — [just closed a funding round of more than $500 million](https://947thebeast.com/2026/10/08/manus-raises-more-than-500-million-after-meta-exit/), its first since resuming life as an independent company. The round was co-led by Boyu Capital and IDG Capital, with existing investors Tencent, HSG and ZhenFund joining in.

Manus itself hasn't disclosed its valuation. But media reports have suggested it could be around $4 billion — [roughly double what Meta agreed to pay](https://theaiinsider.tech/2026/10/08/manus-parent-butterfly-effect-closes-over-500m-in-first-funding-since-collapsed-2b-meta-deal/). Beijing took away the $2 billion check, and the market handed back a company worth twice as much.

## How we got here

If you missed the saga: Manus builds general-purpose AI agents — software that doesn't just answer questions but carries out multi-step tasks on its own, like research, automation and building websites. It went viral in early 2025 with its agent demo, moved its staff to Singapore, and by December had reportedly hit $100 million in annual recurring revenue. Meta swooped in with the acquisition.

Then, in April, China's National Development and Reform Commission [ordered the deal unwound](https://947thebeast.com/2026/10/08/manus-raises-more-than-500-million-after-meta-exit/), saying it would prohibit foreign investment in Manus. Meta — which had already started integrating the company's engineers — had to cut ties. In August, Manus [announced it would operate independently again](https://theaiinsider.tech/2026/10/08/manus-parent-butterfly-effect-closes-over-500m-in-first-funding-since-collapsed-2b-meta-deal/), deleting some user data created after December 2025 to separate its systems from Meta's. Its founders and early investors bought the company back.

It reads like a cautionary tale. In practice it turned into a stress test — one Manus passed.

## It grew into the price tag

The strongest signal isn't the raise; it's the business underneath it. The Information [reported in June](https://947thebeast.com/2026/10/08/manus-raises-more-than-500-million-after-meta-exit/) that Manus's annualised revenue run rate had climbed to around $500 million — five times the roughly $100 million it was pulling in when Meta came calling. For an independent AI company competing against every frontier lab on earth, that's serious commercial traction.

The company hasn't been standing still either. It shipped Manus 2.0, built on a new architecture, and launched Cue, a standalone app that gives personal AI agents their own email addresses, phone numbers, digital wallets and computers. Cue is [a direct play on the same personal-agent territory](https://techxplore.com/news/2026-10-ai-startup-manus-million-meta.html) Meta itself is chasing with its Muse agent — which means Beijing's intervention accidentally created a competitor for the very company it blocked. There's a certain poetry in that.

A Hong Kong listing is reportedly on the table, though [Reuters sources say](https://947thebeast.com/2026/10/08/manus-raises-more-than-500-million-after-meta-exit/) the IPO process won't begin until at least 2027. For now, the company says it will keep hiring in China and abroad.

That product push tells you where Manus thinks the money is. Beyond the chatbot, the company now ships vibe-coding tools for building apps, websites, designs, presentations and video — all generated inside the same agent environment. It's a bet that agents won't stay chat windows for long; they'll become the place work actually gets made. Cue, with its own email, phone number and wallet, is the consumer end of that bet: an agent with an identity, able to transact on your behalf.

## The real takeaway

This deal drama will fade, but the underlying signal matters: investors are still willing to write $500 million cheques to an independent AI agent company, even with Meta, OpenAI, Google and Anthropic all shipping competing agents. As Dan Wang, China director at Eurasia Group, put it: "The fundraising shows that the short-term fallout of the Meta case has been contained and investors are willing to back Manus as an independent company."

Beijing wanted Manus's AI talent and technology to stay in Chinese hands. What it got was an independent company valued at twice Meta's price — one that's now selling the exact product Meta wanted to own. Governments can veto acquisitions. They can't veto ambition.
