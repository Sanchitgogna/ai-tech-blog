Here's a number that will be quoted everywhere this week: 86%. That's the share of small businesses now using AI, according to Goldman Sachs' October 2026 report, [“The AI Economy Is a Small Business Economy.”](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pressroom/press-releases/2026/goldman-sachs-commits-500-million-to-launch-small-business-ai-institute) Two years ago it was 56%. Last year, 77%. The curve looks like a rocket launch.

Now here's the number that actually matters: 15%. That's how many of the surveyed businesses have fully integrated AI into their core operations. Among businesses that already use AI, it's only 18%.

Read those two numbers together and the story flips. Small business hasn't adopted AI. Small business has *sampled* it. Somebody in the office has a chatbot open in a tab. The owner used it to draft a newsletter once. That counts as "using AI" — and by that bar, almost everyone is in. But the thing that changes a business — AI wired into how quotes get written, how stock gets ordered, how customers get answered — has barely started.

Goldman surveyed 2,473 alumni of its *10,000 Small Businesses* programme between August 10 and 24, so treat this as a portrait of motivated, programme-connected owners rather than every shop on every high street. Even with that caveat, the shape of it rings true, because the details underneath are so specific.

The shift in *what* businesses use AI for is the most encouraging part. Two years ago, AI was mostly a content machine: 74% of users employed it for written marketing content. That's now down to 65%, while use for financial analysis and business planning has more than doubled, from 18% to 42%, and data analysis has jumped from 26% to 42%. The toy phase is ending. Owners are pointing the tool at the numbers now, not just the newsletter.

And yet: 74% of AI-using businesses have no formal policy governing how employees use it. Pause on that. Three-quarters of small firms have staff feeding God-knows-what into AI tools with no written rules about what may go in. Customer details, supplier pricing, payroll questions — all of it potentially leaving the building one prompt at a time, with nobody having decided what's allowed. That's not a technology problem. That's a one-page document problem, and almost nobody has written the page.

It gets worse. More than three-quarters of respondents said they could use additional training and resources. So the picture is: everyone has the tool, almost nobody has the training, and three-quarters haven't even set the rules. No wonder only 15% have integrated it. Integration isn't a purchase. It's a decision, followed by training, followed by somebody owning the result.

The good news, such as it is: 93% of AI users report a positive impact, mostly efficiency and time savings. And the job apocalypse hasn't shown up either — 84% of AI-using owners reported no change in headcount, 6% cut staff, 5% added. The technology is doing what its fans promised, just at a much smaller scale than the headlines suggest, because it's being used at the edges instead of the centre.

So who fixes the gap? Goldman, apparently, with help from everyone. On October 22 the bank [launched the Small Business AI Institute](https://www.goldmansachs.com/pressroom/press-releases/2026/goldman-sachs-commits-500-million-to-launch-small-business-ai-institute), part of a $500 million commitment, with Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI all signed on as partners. Google will push its Grow with Google AI Professional certificate to nearly 20,000 owners and employees, including three months of no-cost Google AI Pro. Meta brings Muse for Small Business. OpenAI runs workshops. Anthropic builds a learning series. Four companies that compete ferociously with each other have agreed to share a stage, because they all understand the same thing: the small-business market isn't won by whoever has the best model. It's won by whoever teaches the owner first.

There's something faintly funny about that. The entire AI industry spent two years arguing about which model is smartest, and the decisive battleground turns out to be a training workshop for a florist in Ohio. But that's how technology adoption has always worked. The spreadsheet didn't conquer business because it was clever. It conquered business because somebody showed the bookkeeper how to use it on a Tuesday afternoon.

So here's the takeaway, and it's aimed at anyone running a small business who read the 86% figure and felt behind: you're not behind. You're in the 86%. The race that matters is the 15%, and it's barely started. The way in is almost embarrassingly unglamorous. Pick one workflow that eats your week — quotes, invoicing, answering the same five customer questions. Write a one-page policy saying what staff may and may not put into AI tools. Train one person properly, not everyone superficially. Measure whether the workflow actually got faster.

Do that, and you'll be ahead of roughly seven out of eight businesses that "use AI." The headline number was never the competition. The integration number is.
