Jensen Huang is gone. The AI boom just lost its centre of gravity.

Nvidia's founder resigned on Thursday after 33 years, with no reason given and no permanent successor. The company that powers the AI boom now has five days to prove it can run without the man who was the company.

For 33 years, Nvidia had one chief executive. On Thursday, it had none — at least, none permanent.

The company announced on 12 November that its board had accepted Jensen Huang’s resignation as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993, alongside Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem, and had led it ever since — from a graphics-chip startup to the company whose hardware powers most of the world’s AI. This is the first chief-executive change in the company’s history, and there was no handover, no transition period, no farewell tour.

The filing says what happened. It does not say why.

What the filing actually says

Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim CEO, keeping her finance job at the same time. She has been Nvidia’s CFO since September 2013, and spent close to 25 years in finance roles at Cisco and Microsoft before that. The board also named Harvey C. Jones — a former chief executive and executive chairman of chip-design firm Synopsys — as its first-ever board chair. Nvidia has never had an outside chair before; it got one on the same day its only CEO stepped aside.

Jones said the board has “full confidence” in Kress and the existing leadership team, and expects “the company’s operations, customer commitments and product roadmap to continue without interruption.” The board will consider internal and external candidates for the permanent job. No timeline was given.

The first test arrives fast. Nvidia reports third-quarter fiscal 2027 results on Tuesday, 17 November, with the earnings call at 2 p.m. Pacific. It will be Kress’s first public appearance as interim chief executive, five days after taking the job. The market’s first verdict was not gentle: shares plunged as much as 14% in early trading on Thursday, according to the Associated Press, before closing down more than 4%.

What the filing doesn’t say

Everything else about this resignation comes from reporting, not from Nvidia — and the reporting is extraordinary. The New York Times reported that Huang had an affair with a businesswoman, Alice Huang (also known as Huang Le), a former executive at Singapore data-centre company Megaspeed International, whom US intelligence agencies concluded was feeding information directly to Beijing. Beijing denies it; China’s Foreign Ministry called the spying allegations “unsubstantiated” and a smear campaign. The two Huangs are not related.

Over the weekend, Huang apologised to employees in an email, saying he had been “unfaithful to my wife”. Nvidia said on Sunday it was investigating whether Huang had been “the target of a foreign intelligence effort”, while adding it had “no reason to believe” that national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised. Then, late on Wednesday, reports emerged of a second affair, this time with an Nvidia executive who reported to him directly. Reuters later reported the same, citing unidentified sources. The resignation arrived shortly after. Nvidia’s announcement addressed neither relationship.

Donald Trump said the FBI is looking into the affair. He also maintained that Huang had “done a fantastic job”, and offered his own succession advice on Truth Social: former Apple chief Tim Cook. “There is only one man to take his place,” he wrote. “Legend replaces a Legend!”

The resignation, the interim appointment, the chair and the earnings date are all confirmed in Nvidia’s own SEC filing. The affair, the intelligence conclusions and the FBI probe are reported by named outlets, denied in part by Beijing, and not addressed by Nvidia at all. Treat the two categories differently, because they are.

Why this is bigger than one company

Nvidia is not just a chipmaker. Its accelerators are the bottleneck the entire AI industry queues behind, and Huang was not just its CEO — he was its public face, its chief lobbyist in Washington, and the person who personally argued for looser AI regulation and looser chip-export limits to China. On the same day Nvidia announced the resignation, AMD unveiled the Instinct MI450X accelerator with 288 GB of memory, and a consortium of rival chipmakers and cloud firms — including AMD, Intel, Google and Meta — launched the SGLang Consortium to coordinate open inference software.

There is also a quieter question, one this blog has followed all year. Huang was Nvidia’s most visible advocate for open-weight AI — the Nemotron model family and, most pointedly, the reported $12.9 billion acquisition of Hugging Face, confirmed in September and still not closed. That advocacy was closely identified with Huang personally, not codified as board policy, and neither Kress nor Jones addressed open-source strategy in the filing. Beyond Jones’s general assurance that the roadmap continues “without interruption”, teams building on Nemotron have no new guidance.

Forrester’s Stephanie Balaouras put the stakes in the clearest terms of anyone quoted so far: “If Nvidia stabilises quickly under new leadership, the AI infrastructure boom will have demonstrated resilience. If its stock, financing relationships, or ecosystem commitments remain under sustained pressure, that would add evidence to the fragile side of the AI bubble ledger.”

That is the honest frame. The AI boom has always had a single point of failure, and it was never a chip. It was a man in a leather jacket who was the company, the strategy and the sales pitch all at once. Tuesday’s earnings call will tell us whether the machine he built can run without him. Five days is not a long time to find out.
