Norway wants to ban AI glasses in public — and it's asking the question nobody wants to answer

The first country-level ban proposal isn't about the tech. It's about what happens when the tech becomes invisible.

On Monday, Norway’s government did something no country has done before: it said it plans to temporarily ban AI smart glasses in public places.

Not all glasses, and not everywhere. The proposal — a bill to be put before Norway’s parliament, the Storting, “as soon as possible” — would restrict camera-equipped glasses in places like parks, beaches, museums, shopping centres, schools, kindergartens, hospitals, and gym changing rooms. Private use at home stays legal. Digitalisation Minister Torgeir Micaelsen framed it as a pause, not a prohibition: time for an expert panel to assess the technology and hold what he called an “enlightened debate” before permanent rules are written. (Cybernews)

The question he’s asking is the one the rest of us keep dodging: what happens to public life when anyone near you might be recording it?

The camera you can’t see

This isn’t a new worry — it’s an old worry wearing a new form factor. Smartphones already film everything. CCTV already watches every shopping centre. The difference with AI glasses is the absence of the social cue. When someone raises a phone, you see the phone. When someone taps record on a pair of Meta Ray-Bans — and Meta has sold over 9 million pairs since early 2023 — the only signal is a small LED that you probably won’t notice across a playground.

That’s the technical detail that makes this a regulatory problem rather than an etiquette one. A place-based ban is straightforward to write and genuinely hard to enforce. The glasses are, by design, indistinguishable from ordinary eyewear. Norway’s law would rely on people noticing, reporting, and venues acting — a system that works only if everyone cooperates, which is roughly the assumption every unenforceable rule starts with.

Meta’s counter-argument is that the hardware already handles this: a front LED that lights up during recording and disables the camera if covered. Privacy groups like Privacy International have argued the tiny light isn’t enough. And they’re probably right in the narrow sense — a blinking pinhead of light is not meaningful consent — but notice what’s happening here: we’re arguing about the size of an LED because we’ve already accepted that the camera is going to be on people’s faces.

The world is already doing this, piecemeal

Norway’s proposal doesn’t exist in a vacuum. It’s just the first national government to try.

In France, child welfare groups documented around 20 reports this year of women being filmed without consent with camera glasses, in incidents described as social media pranks. Last week, California Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed a bill that would have penalised covert smart-glass recording in private spaces — not because he disagrees with the principle, but because the bill’s definition of a “wearable recording device” was considered too broad. In the Netherlands, the eyewear retailer Hans Anders suspended Ray-Ban Meta sales entirely while waiting for regulatory guidance. UK cinemas and pub chains have their own restrictions.

So the pattern is: private venues ban them, retailers flinch, regulators draft, and then the drafts either die or stall. Norway is the first to say “we’re doing this at national level,” and even they need opposition support to get it through a parliament where the government holds only a minority.

The carve-outs matter more than the ban

The most interesting part of the Norwegian proposal isn’t what’s banned — it’s what’s exempted. The government says it will build in exceptions for vulnerable groups and “socially beneficial uses,” including accessibility tools like real-time translation and navigation assistance.

This is exactly right, and exactly where the hard trade-offs live. The same camera-plus-AI combination that lets someone secretly film a stranger in a changing room lets a blind person read a restaurant menu. Any permanent regulation has to draw a line between a recording device and an assistive one — and that line is going to be argued over for years, because the devices are literally identical. The difference is intent, and intent doesn’t show up on a hardware spec sheet.

Norway’s temporary approach is the smart version of this: admit you don’t know how to draw the line yet, pause, and give an expert group time to figure it out. The alternative — which California just demonstrated — is writing a law, discovering the definition is unworkable, and vetoing it.

What to actually watch

The ban, if it passes, will be imperfect and hard to enforce. That’s not really the point. The point is the precedent: the first national government treating always-on wearable cameras as a category of technology that needs its own rules, not just an extension of phone-camera etiquette.

Two things to watch now. First, whether other governments follow or watch from the sidelines — the UK’s data regulator has reportedly been questioning Meta over the same concerns since March, and a live Norwegian example gives every regulator cover to act. Second, whether the enforcement problem forces a hardware answer: bigger recording indicators, mandatory audible cues, or OS-level rules that platforms control. If place-based bans can’t be policed, the next proposal won’t be about where you wear the glasses. It’ll be about how the glasses themselves are built.

Until then, Norway has named the problem louder than anyone else: we are sleepwalking into a world where the social contract of “I can see you’re filming me” no longer applies. The glasses will get better, cheaper, and more common. The question of who gets to record whom — and what anyone can do about it — is only going to get louder.
