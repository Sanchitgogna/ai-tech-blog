Anthropic just filed to go public. Its prospectus is the most honest document in AI.

$16.3 billion of revenue, an $80.7 billion loss, and a warning, in its own paperwork, that its products could cause catastrophic harm.

Every AI company tells you a story. This week, Anthropic had to file one under penalty of perjury.

On Monday 26 October, the company behind Claude published its S-1 registration statement with the US Securities and Exchange Commission, the document that starts its journey to a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT. It is the first time the public has seen detailed financial statements from a frontier AI lab, and the numbers are absurd in both directions at once: $16.3 billion of revenue in the first half of 2026, against a net loss of $80.7 billion.

Start with the loss, because it is the number everyone will quote and almost nobody will understand. Roughly $72.4 billion of it is a non-cash accounting charge: the rising value of convertible notes and other financing instruments that can turn into Anthropic shares. It is not money the company spent. Strip it out and the picture changes completely. Anthropic’s operating loss actually narrowed, to $693 million from $2.1 billion a year earlier, while revenue grew roughly fourteen-fold, from $1.16 billion in the first half of 2025.

That is the good news. The bad news is underneath it — and it is why this filing matters more than any product launch this month.

Anthropic spent nearly $9 billion on computing and infrastructure in those six months, up from $2.3 billion a year earlier. And according to reporting on the filing, it has future minimum payment obligations of about $245 billion, much of it in compute agreements with Google and Amazon — both of which are also Anthropic investors. Amazon alone holds $6.2 billion of Anthropic convertible debt and has agreed to provide up to $15 billion in financing, in three $5 billion tranches. Read that twice: Anthropic’s biggest lender is also one of its biggest suppliers, and its biggest customer base runs on its lenders’ clouds.

This is the economics of frontier AI laid bare. Revenue is exploding, costs are exploding faster in absolute terms, and the whole machine is financed by promises that stretch years into the future. The filing’s risk section says so in the open: Anthropic warns prospective investors that advanced AI models could cause serious real-world harm, including scenarios it describes as catastrophic, and that the company may never reach or sustain profitability. Most prospectuses warn that a company might fail. Few warn that its product might end the world.

Then there is the governance. Anthropic is listing as a public benefit corporation, and its seven co-founders — including chief executive Dario Amodei and president Daniela Amodei — will retain 50.1% of voting power on general shareholder matters after the offering. A separate Long-Term Benefit Trust will have the authority to appoint a majority of the board.

The timing is not an accident either. Anthropic confidentially filed its draft prospectus back on 1 June; going public now means the roadshow runs while the numbers still look like a rocket ship: as of 30 September, more than 11,900 customers each generated at least $100,000 of revenue over the previous twelve months, including more than 1,900 generating at least $1 million. That customer base is exactly what OpenAI has been discounting furiously to poach — which makes this filing, in part, a counter-move: Anthropic showing its customers, and its rival, that it can stand on its own balance sheet.

What is still missing tells you as much as what is there. No price range, no share count, no listing date. Media reports point to a debut in the second half of November, possibly the week of 16 November, with the deal potentially raising $100 billion or more. Reported valuation chatter ranges from $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion — down from the $2 trillion floated a couple of months ago, and up sharply from the $965 billion private valuation of its May funding round. None of those figures comes from the company, and the gap between them is itself a signal: nobody, including the people arranging the deal, quite knows what this thing is worth.

The market’s first reaction was enthusiasm: US chip stocks gained 2.8% on Monday, their best day in a month according to Reuters, and on Tuesday South Korea’s KOSPI rose more than 4% in early trade. Investors read the filing as a green light for continued AI infrastructure spending — a reasonable read, and also the most dangerous one, since the same document discloses the $245 billion of obligations already signed to keep it going.

So here is the honest summary of the most honest document in AI. Anthropic is growing faster than almost any company in history, losing money on paper at a scale that would bankrupt a country, actually improving its underlying operations, and committing itself to a quarter of a trillion dollars of future payments to two companies that also own chunks of it — all while warning, in writing, that it might never be profitable and that its technology could cause catastrophic harm.

Strange thing to buy into — and, for the first time, a thing you will soon be able to buy into. The amended filings, when they set a price, will tell us whether the public market prices the rocket ship, the warning label, or both.
