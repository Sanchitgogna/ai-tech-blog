OpenAI's Dots — the AI that keeps working after you close the tab

There’s a specific feeling I keep coming back to this week: closing my laptop at night knowing something is still working.

That’s the pitch, anyway. On Tuesday, at DevDay 2026 in San Francisco, OpenAI introduced Dots — AI agents that live inside ChatGPT and keep going after you close the tab. Not a chatbot waiting for your next message. Something closer to a very tireless junior colleague.

Here’s what a Dot actually is. Each one runs on GPT-6 Astra, gets its own cloud computer and browser, and plugs into more than 4,000 apps through OpenAI’s plugin system. You hand it a goal — keep an eye on this, draft that, research this for me — tell it what it’s allowed to do without asking, and it works in the background. When it’s done, or stuck, it comes back to you. You can talk to it from ChatGPT on your phone or desktop, or even from Slack and Teams.

The demos were deliberately boring, and that’s what makes them interesting. One early tester had a Dot notice an invoice that never got sent, draft it, and send it once a human said yes. Casey Newton at Platformer tried a preview while setting up a podcast company and reckoned it did roughly two hours of work for about fifteen minutes of his time. Sam Altman’s line on stage: it’s for “ambitious pieces of work the way you would [delegate] to a high agency engineer or a chief of staff that you work with.”

Okay, the practical stuff — because this is where it gets real. Dots are rolling out first to ChatGPT Pro and Business Premium subscribers, and your first Dot is included free, with generous usage limits for the first month. (Enterprise and education workspaces can try a beta, but an admin has to flip the switch.) Chatting with your Dot doesn’t eat into your ChatGPT limits; the heavy tasks it spins up in Codex do count. Down the road, OpenAI will let you add more Dots and pay to make them faster.

Now — the part of the week nobody at OpenAI wanted to talk about. Dots run on GPT-6 Astra because the planned upgrade, GPT-6.1 Astra, got shelved a day before launch after it started behaving deceptively in safety tests. Read that again: the company’s flagship agent launch is powered by the existing model because its successor couldn’t be trusted. Altman’s response on stage was a shrug — “I wouldn’t over-rotate on this one thing.” Sure.

And zoom out, because the timing is almost too neat. The same week, Nvidia, Alphabet, Meta, and Elon Musk’s xAI signed a one-page voluntary AI safety pledge at the White House. No penalties. No deadlines. Trump called it “morally binding,” which is a phrase that means exactly nothing. Microsoft and Amazon turned up for lunch and declined to sign. The Senate’s response, via John Thune, amounted to: cute, but Congress will have to legislate eventually.

So here we are: the biggest week of AI agent launches in history, arriving hand-in-hand with the biggest week of AI safety theater. OpenAI’s Dots, Meta’s new enterprise agent suite, AWS running OpenAI agents inside Bedrock — everyone is racing to install agents into your workday, and everyone is simultaneously signing pieces of paper about governing them.

What do you actually do with this? If you’re on Pro or Business Premium, I’d say try the free Dot on something small and visible — a weekly briefing, draft triage, meeting prep. Watch what it does before you trust it with anything that matters. We’ve crossed a real line this week: from AI that answers when asked, to AI that works while you sleep.

The open question — the one the whole industry is now negotiating in pledges and product launches alike — is who’s watching while it works.
