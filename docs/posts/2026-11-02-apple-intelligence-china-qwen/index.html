Apple's AI finally arrives in China — running on someone else's model

After two years without it, Chinese iPhones get Apple Intelligence in December — powered by Alibaba's Qwen, and a generation behind everyone else.

For two years, Apple has sold iPhones in China with a hole in the middle of the pitch. Everywhere else, Apple Intelligence was the reason to upgrade: the smarter Siri, the writing help, the camera that understands what it is looking at. In China, none of it arrived. On Saturday, Apple finally closed the gap — sort of.

Apple announced on its China newsroom that Apple Intelligence will come to mainland China in December with iOS 27.3, with testing in the latest iOS developer beta from 4 November. The catch, and it is a big one, is that the brains behind it will not be Apple’s. They will be Alibaba’s Qwen.

Outside China, Siri hands questions it cannot answer to OpenAI’s ChatGPT, and the rebuilt Siri AI is built with Google’s Gemini. In China, those same surfaces — Siri, Writing Tools and Visual Intelligence — will call on Qwen instead, for text and image understanding and content generation. A Compose option in Writing Tools lets Qwen write original text from scratch anywhere in the system, and you can ask it about photos and documents, including PDFs and presentations.

This is not a small footnote. It means Apple Intelligence is no longer quite one product. It is a brand with a different engine underneath depending on where you stand. The interface looks the same, the privacy promises sound the same, but the model answering your question changes at the border.

What Chinese users actually get in December is the first generation of Apple Intelligence, not the new one. The package includes Live Translation in Messages, FaceTime and the Phone app, Writing Tools for rewriting and summarising, Visual Intelligence through the camera, Clean Up in Photos, Genmoji, Priority Messages and notification summaries, and Smart Reply. Siri gets a more natural voice and Type to Siri.

What it does not include is the thing Apple has spent this year talking about most: Siri AI, the rebuilt assistant that arrived with iOS 27 elsewhere, and the other next-generation features announced at WWDC 2026. Apple says so plainly. So the users who waited longest get the oldest version — worth holding onto while the press releases do their work.

There are two reassurances attached, and both are worth reading carefully. First, the Qwen integration is opt-in: users choose whether to switch it on and what information to share, and they sign in with a Qwen account or with Sign in with Apple. Second, Alibaba says it will not use user requests or generated responses to train its models. That matters: the obvious question about handing Siri queries to a second company is what it learns from them. On paper, nothing — but only the beta and the months after it can show whether that holds.

Eligibility is narrower than “every iPhone in China”. You need an iPhone 16 or later, an iPhone Duo, an iPhone 15 Pro or Pro Max, or an iPhone Air, with Siri and the device language set to Simplified Chinese or English. You must either have bought an Apple Intelligence-capable iPhone in China or have both your device location and your Apple Account country or region set to China. It is iPhone-only at first; iPad and Mac support follows only after further regulatory approvals.

Why did it take this long? The short answer is regulation. China requires consumer generative AI services to be approved before launch, and the partnership itself has been public for a while: Alibaba’s chairman confirmed in February 2025 that Apple had shopped around among Chinese companies before choosing Alibaba. The logjam broke in July, when China’s cyberspace authority filed seven on-device generative AI services for smartphones, including Apple Intelligence. From first confirmation to first beta, that is more than a year and a half of waiting — most of it spent on paperwork, not engineering.

That timeline is the real story here. Apple did not spend two years building a China-specific assistant. It spent two years getting permission to ship one built by someone else. And the permission it got covers the old assistant, not the new one, which means the regulatory clock is probably still ticking for Siri AI in China. Nobody has said when that version arrives, or what model would power it if it did.

There is also a conspicuous gap in the announcement: neither company has said which Qwen model sits behind the features. Qwen is a family, not a single model, and the difference between its members is the difference between a good answer and a shrug. Until testers get their hands on the beta on 4 November, we are being asked to take “Qwen” on trust.

So watch the beta. Watch how clearly Apple explains the permission prompt when Siri wants to send a question to Qwen — “opt-in” can mean a thoughtful choice or a single tap on a screen full of defaults. Watch whether Qwen’s answers in Chinese feel native or translated, and whether Visual Intelligence copes with real photos rather than demo ones. And watch whether anyone, anywhere, explains why the newest Siri is still missing.

Apple’s China problem was never really about technology. It was about whether one phone can carry one AI assistant worldwide. The answer, it turns out, is no: in China, Apple Intelligence arrives at last as a local product wearing a global name, a generation late, with someone else’s model doing the thinking.
