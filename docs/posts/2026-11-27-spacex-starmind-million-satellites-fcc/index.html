The FCC just cleared a million AI satellites. The sky is now a data centre.

SpaceX's Starmind got the first-ever US licence for orbital data centres. The hard parts — cooling, debris, launch cadence — are all still unsolved.

One million satellites. Not for broadband, not for Earth observation — for running AI.

On Wednesday, the Federal Communications Commission authorised SpaceX to deploy up to a million satellites for its Starmind constellation, a fleet of solar-powered orbital data centres meant to process AI workloads above the atmosphere and send the results back down. It is, by the FCC’s own description, the first authorisation in US history for a constellation of data centres in space.

The scale is genuinely hard to picture. SpaceX’s Starlink — already the largest satellite constellation ever built — has just over 11,000 satellites in orbit. Starmind would increase the total number of satellites in Earth orbit roughly fifty-fold. The satellites would fly at altitudes of 500 to 1,002 kilometres, talk to each other by laser, and fall back on radio links only if the lasers fail.

Why space, and why now

The pitch, in SpaceX’s words, is “capturing solar energy in space to power low-cost, high-performance AI compute for Earth.” Up there, the sun never sets, nobody complains about the power bill, and cooling — a data centre’s eternal headache — becomes a different problem rather than the same one.

It’s also a pitch that lands at a useful political moment. Ground-based data centres have become a genuine source of public backlash: land use, water, electricity prices, the hum of a new neighbour the size of a warehouse district. That backlash showed up in this month’s midterm elections. Moving the servers to orbit neatly sidesteps every local planning meeting in America. FCC Chair Brendan Carr, announcing the decision, said it “opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America’s leadership and growth.” The FCC’s own release went further, claiming Starmind “promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization” — a civilisation that harnesses the full power of its sun. Musk thanked Carr on X: “This will be a great asset to America and the world.”

SpaceX filed its application on 30 January. In August it announced a partnership with Nvidia to design the compute payload — each Starmind satellite is to carry Nvidia Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs. Its chief financial officer, Bret Johnsen, said last week the company plans to launch its first orbital data centres in 2027 and expand through 2029. The FCC’s licence is more patient: the first operational satellite by November 2033, 100,000 of them by 2035, half the constellation by 2038, all one million by 2040.

The objections, and what the FCC did with them

Not everyone is applauding. Amazon — which is building its own Starlink competitor — called Starmind “a lofty ambition rather than a real plan” in its filing, and warned the proposal “risks real harm.” Viasat objected too. The American Astronomical Society, which raised concerns about the constellation’s effect on ground-based astronomy, said it was “disheartened and dismayed” by the decision. The Secure World Foundation urged regulators to assess the cumulative risks to orbital sustainability.

The FCC dismissed some of those concerns on the grounds that they fall outside its regulatory authority, and declined to undertake a comprehensive environmental assessment. It also didn’t give SpaceX everything it asked for: the company wanted a waiver so satellites operating above 600 kilometres could be parked in graveyard orbits at the end of their lives instead of burning up in the atmosphere. The FCC said SpaceX hadn’t provided enough information about its disposal plan. Current rules require satellites ending missions below 2,000 kilometres to re-enter within five years; SpaceX has floated moving retired satellites into other orbits — or even into orbit around the sun.

That’s the detail that gives the game away. A million satellites, each with a chip that needs cooling in a vacuum, built and launched at a cadence no rocket fleet has ever achieved, financed with chips that have to survive the trip — and the plan for what happens when they die is still “we’ll get back to you.” TechCrunch’s verdict was blunt: this approval doesn’t mean orbital data centres are imminent. Gizmodo noted the whole scheme leans heavily on Starship, SpaceX’s giant rocket, for a launch cadence and payload capacity far beyond what conventional rockets can provide economically.

A race is already forming

SpaceX isn’t the only one looking up. Blue Origin has proposed a 51,600-satellite constellation called Project Sunrise. Starcloud has applied for 88,000 satellites. Cowboy Space — formerly Aetherflux — wants 20,000. Google is testing satellites for Project Suncatcher, with its first launches planned on Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket no earlier than 2029. The FCC says it’s still reviewing the other proposals.

So this isn’t one company’s moonshot. It’s the opening move in a new land grab — except the land is low Earth orbit, and the prize is where the world’s AI runs. Ground data centres need land, power grids, water, permits and neighbours who don’t mind. Orbital ones need a launch industry that doesn’t exist yet at this scale, chips that work in a vacuum, and a debris plan that nobody has written.

The FCC has now said yes to the idea. The physics, the economics and the night sky get their say next — and they don’t take meetings.
