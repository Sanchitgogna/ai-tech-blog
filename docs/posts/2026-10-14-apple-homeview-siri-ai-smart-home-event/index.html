Apple just put a $449 screen in your kitchen. It knows your face.

Welcome Home" was Apple's smart-home coming-out party — and the fine print says as much as the hardware.

Yesterday, Apple held a product launch that wasn’t really a launch. No livestream, no filmed keynote — just journalists filing into a venue in Tribeca, New York, for a hands-on experience called “Welcome Home.” The name was the message: after years of letting Amazon and Google run the living room, Apple wants its AI to live in your house, not just your pocket.

So out came three new boxes, all orbiting one idea: the upgraded Siri AI is now a home assistant first.

The main event: a screen with opinions about your family

The headliner is the HomeView, Apple’s first dedicated smart display — a 7-inch Liquid Retina touchscreen on a speaker base, powered by the A18 chip. It comes in a tabletop version and a wall-mounted one, both at US$299 (AU$449 RRP, pre-order open now via apple.com/au, on sale 13 November).

HomeView’s party trick is personalisation. It recognises up to six people by face and serves each of them their own screens, calendars, reminders and messages. It handles FaceTime calls with a 12MP Centre Stage camera, pulls up to nine camera feeds at once, and controls the usual suspects — lights, thermostats, locks. Apple says it’s “designed to be central to the home and personal to everyone in it.”

Pause on that for a second. A camera on your kitchen bench that identifies every family member by face, linked to your messages and calendar. Apple’s pitch is that all of this happens privately, on-device and on Apple’s terms. You don’t have to buy the trust — but you do have to pay for it, at roughly twice the cost of the Google and Amazon equivalents. This is Apple doing what Apple does: charging a premium to be the private option.

The speaker that arrives half-smart

Then there’s the HomePod mini 2 — AU$229 RRP, in shops from 30 October, five new colours, S10 chip, Wi-Fi 6E, Thread and Matter, and a “Sound Glow” top that pulses with the music. It’s a genuinely better small speaker. It can now serve as the default speaker for your Mac, and four of them pair with the new Apple TV 4K for surround sound.

Here’s the catch: the reason any of us would upgrade a smart speaker — the new Siri AI — doesn’t ship on it. Apple says it will “get Siri AI support with future software updates.” No date. So the product you can pre-order today, arriving in two weeks, is shipping its headline feature as an IOU. Apple is asking you to buy the hardware on faith that the software catches up. That’s a lot of trust for a company that promised a smarter Siri years ago and is only now delivering.

The country that doesn’t get one

Here’s my favourite detail of the whole event: HomeView is not being sold in the EU at all. Per Apple’s own fine print, Siri AI “will not be initially available in the EU in iOS or iPadOS” — so Apple simply isn’t selling the device there. Only eight English-language countries get it, and they’re exactly the eight where Siri AI works.

On one hand, that’s admirable restraint: no selling a brainless smart screen. On the other, it’s quietly hilarious that the strongest privacy jurisdiction on earth can’t buy Apple’s flagship privacy pitch. Europe will have to watch the rest of us talk to our kitchens.

The subscription hiding in the fine print

And then there’s the line in Apple’s small print that nobody on stage read out: Apple Intelligence features that use server-side models have daily usage limits, and “expanded access to such features will be available for a fee in the future.” That’s from coverage of Apple’s own documentation.

Let that sit. The company that has always sold you hardware is telling you, in the footnotes, that the assistant’s full abilities may eventually cost a subscription. Maybe it’s nothing — contingency language lawyers write for fun. But if your $449 kitchen screen starts rationing its intelligence and asking for a monthly top-up, that’s the moment Apple stops being the company that sells you the box and becomes the company that rents you the brain.

So should you care?

The Apple TV 4K refresh (A19 chip, three far-field microphones, Find My remote, US$299, out 13 November) is the least dramatic box in the lineup, but it might be the most strategic: it’s the hub the other two plug into. And the new Apple Home Collection — LG and Schneider doorbells, locks, cameras, thermostats and sensors arriving from December — is Apple’s first real “second-party” hardware push. Apple designed the ecosystem; partners build the bits. It’s the company admitting it can’t outfit a whole house alone.

Here’s my honest read. Apple is betting that the smart home’s next decade is decided by trust, not specs — and it’s charging accordingly. The hardware is good, the prices are Apple-prices, and the personalisation pitch is genuinely ambitious. But one flagship arrives half-smart, the EU can’t buy it, and the fine print whispers “subscription.” If you want a camera that knows your kids’ faces in exchange for Siri that actually works, fine. Just know you’re buying the promise, not only the product.
